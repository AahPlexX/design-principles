Level 3, Lesson 3: Optical alignment vs. mathematical alignment

A developer centers a triangular "play" icon inside a circular button using exact math — the triangle's bounding box lands precisely on the circle's center point. On screen, the triangle looks like it's sitting slightly too far to the left, even though the numbers say otherwise.

A triangular play icon is centered inside a circular button using exact bounding-box math, but designers almost always nudge the triangle a few pixels to the right of that calculated center. Why?

"Mathematical alignment" is what a ruler or a precise CSS measurement would confirm as centered — equal pixels on every side of a bounding box. "Optical alignment" is adjusting a shape's position based on how it actually looks to the eye, because some shapes distribute their visual weight unevenly inside their own box. A triangle's flat edge sits on one side and its point on the other, so its "mass" reads as concentrated away from the point — centering it by the numbers leaves it looking off-balance, and nudging it a few pixels toward the point fixes the appearance. The same idea shows up with bold letters at the start of a headline (a capital "T" sometimes needs a small negative margin because its strokes make it look like it starts further right than the text below it) and with round shapes like circles and "O"s, which often need to sit very slightly larger or offset to look the same size as square-edged neighbors.
