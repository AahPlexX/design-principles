Level 1, Lesson 2: Similarity — same-styled items read as one group

A navigation bar lists five items — Home, Products, Pricing, Log in, Sign up — each separated by the same 16px gap. Nobody laid out the gaps unevenly, yet users still glance at it and treat "Log in" and "Sign up" as a pair, distinct from the other three. Equal spacing didn't cause that grouping — something else did.

The navbar's five items all sit 16px apart, with no extra gap anywhere in the row. "Log in" and "Sign up" are both rendered as outlined buttons; "Home," "Products," and "Pricing" are plain text links. Why do "Log in" and "Sign up" read as one group even though the spacing is identical across the whole row?

When spacing can't do the grouping job — because every gap in a row is equal — shared styling takes over. Elements that look alike, whether that's the same shape, color, or type of control, get read as belonging together even with identical space around each one. That's why the two buttons form their own visual unit inside a navbar that otherwise has uniform spacing.
