Level 1, Lesson 4: A screen can lie about contrast

A contrast ratio is math done on a color's underlying values — it doesn't know or care what monitor is displaying it. Two people can look at the exact same hex codes on two different screens and walk away with two different opinions about whether the text is readable, while the actual ratio hasn't moved at all.

A designer, working on a bright laptop display with the brightness and contrast turned up, styles secondary card text as #999999 on a white (#ffffff) background. On her screen it looks perfectly readable. A teammate runs it through a contrast checker later and it fails, scoring roughly 2.85:1 — well under the 4.5:1 minimum for body text. Both of them are looking at the identical hex values. What explains the disagreement?

#999999 on white sits at roughly 2.85:1 no matter which screen it's viewed on — that number comes from the colors themselves, not from the display showing them. A bright, high-contrast laptop screen in good lighting can make a fail look fine to the eye; a dimmer screen, an older eye, or direct sunlight will expose the same fail immediately. That's exactly why you measure the ratio with a tool instead of trusting how any one screen renders it.
