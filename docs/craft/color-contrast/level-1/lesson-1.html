Level 1, Lesson 1: Luminance, not RGB distance

It's tempting to compare two hex codes the way you'd compare two phone numbers — subtract the digits and see how far apart they are. Contrast ratio isn't measuring that. It's measuring how bright each color actually reads to a human eye, and that brightness weighs each color channel differently — so two color pairs with the exact same numeric "distance" between them can land on completely different ratios.

A designer is picking a text color for a dark background and wants to compare two candidates quickly: pure blue (#0000FF) and a matching-brightness green (#008000). Subtracting each candidate's RGB values from the black background's (0, 0, 0) gives a similar-looking "distance" for both. The designer assumes both will score about the same on a contrast checker. Why might the checker return very different ratios instead?

Contrast ratio comes from relative luminance — a measure of perceived brightness built by weighting the red, green, and blue channels separately, because the human eye doesn't perceive equal amounts of each color as equally bright. Green at a given value reads as noticeably brighter than blue at that same value, so two colors that look "equally far" from black in raw RGB terms can produce very different contrast ratios once that weighting is applied.
