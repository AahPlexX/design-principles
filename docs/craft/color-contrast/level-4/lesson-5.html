Level 4, Lesson 5: Distinguishing adjacent chart categories without relying on hue alone

Status colors are usually a small, fixed set — four or five values you can hand-pick for lightness separation. Charts are a harder case: a stacked bar chart or a multi-line graph can have many categories, often generated from an automatic palette, and once you're past a handful of colors, some neighboring hues are going to land close enough to collide under a color vision deficiency no matter how carefully the palette was built. At that point the fix isn't a better palette — it's giving each category a second, non-color way to be told apart: a direct label, a distinct line pattern, or a texture on the fill.

A stacked bar chart has six categories drawn from an auto-generated color palette. Two of the middle categories are both muted teal-ish tones that render as the same color once simulated for a red-green deficiency. What's the most direct fix?

Once a palette has enough categories that some neighbors are going to land close together under some pattern of color vision deficiency, re-tuning the palette alone doesn't reliably fix it — brightening or shifting the whole set can just move the collision to a different pair of categories. Giving each category an independent, non-color signal, like a label placed directly on it or a distinct pattern, keeps it readable even if two colors still collide.
