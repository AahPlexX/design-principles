Level 2, Lesson 3: Why large text gets a lower bar — stroke width, letterform recognition

The 3:1 threshold for large text isn't a rounding-down of 4.5:1 out of pity for headings — it comes from how reading actually works at that size. A letter's strokes take up more physical space on screen the bigger the text gets, so even a fainter color still puts down enough pixels to read as a solid shape instead of a thin, breakable line. At smaller sizes, a stroke is only a pixel or two wide, so the same faint color can visually dissolve into the background — turning a letter into a smudge instead of a shape.

There's a second reason on top of that: at large sizes, your eye recognizes a whole letterform's outline — its overall shape — rather than needing to resolve every fine detail inside it. That's a lower bar to clear than reading small text, where you're relying on much finer detail to tell one letter from another. Neither reason has anything to do with how "important" the text is — it's about what the eye can still resolve at that size.

A designer says: "Large headings can use lower contrast because they're less important than body copy, so it's fine if they fade a bit." What's wrong with this reasoning?

Large text gets a lower minimum because thicker strokes and bigger, more recognizable shapes are physically easier for the eye to resolve at a fainter color — not because the text matters less. A large, unimportant label and a large, critical warning both get the same 3:1 floor; a small, critical warning still needs 4.5:1.
