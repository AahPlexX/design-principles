Level 3, Lesson 3: Fewer content-driven breakpoints vs. adding extras "just in case"

Once a team learns to place breakpoints where content actually breaks, a second temptation shows up: adding a handful of extra breakpoints nobody asked for, "just to be safe." Every breakpoint is CSS someone has to read, test, and keep in sync later — a breakpoint that isn't fixing an actual problem is pure cost with nothing to show for it.

A three-column card layout looks fine at every width from 320px up to 1600px, except at 768px, where the columns get too cramped and need to drop to two. A teammate suggests adding four more breakpoints anyway — at 480px, 600px, 900px, and 1200px — "just in case some device needs it." What's the problem with adding those extra breakpoints?

The layout already handles every width from 320px to 1600px except the one point where it needs a breakpoint. The four extra breakpoints don't fix anything — they add rules to maintain, and more places for a future change to drift out of sync, at widths where nothing was actually going wrong. A breakpoint earns its place by fixing an observed problem in this layout; "just in case" isn't a reason, it's a guess wearing a reason's clothes.
