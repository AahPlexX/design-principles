Level 3, Lesson 4: Strong hierarchy overrides the default scan pattern

The F-pattern, the Z-pattern, and the Gutenberg diagram all describe the same situation: what an eye does by default when nothing on the page is actively competing for its attention. None of them are a law of nature. The instant one element gets real emphasis — a much bigger headline, a single saturated button, an image sitting alone in open space — the eye breaks from whatever default path it was tracing and jumps straight to the loud thing first. Emphasis outranks position every time.

A long-form article (normally an F-pattern page) has, halfway down its right-hand margin — the area an F-pattern reader typically skips entirely — a bright red banner reading "Limited-time offer," set in large bold white text, far bigger and higher-contrast than anything else on the page. Will the F-pattern still cause most readers to miss it?

Scan patterns like the F-pattern describe default behavior on a low-hierarchy page — they don't override a genuinely strong emphasis signal. A banner with far more size, color, and contrast than anything around it functions as a much higher-priority instruction to the eye than its position on the page does, so it gets noticed even sitting in a spot the default pattern usually skips. Where an element sits in the HTML has nothing to do with this — it's what the eye sees, not the order it was written in.
