Level 3, Lesson 5: Focal points and directional cues

Sometimes the strongest hierarchy signal isn't size or color at all — it's a direction. A photographed person's gaze, an arrow, a road receding into the distance, a hand pointing: these are directional cues (the ones drawn as literal lines in a photo or illustration are often called leading lines), and eye-tracking studies find that people reliably look where they point, almost like following an invisible arrow.

A signup page has a large photo of a person next to the signup form. Version A: the person faces the camera dead-on, looking straight out at the reader. Version B: the person is turned slightly, looking toward the form instead. Which version is more likely to get a first-time visitor's attention onto the form faster?

A pictured person's gaze functions as a directional cue the same way an arrow or a converging line does — viewers' eyes tend to follow it toward whatever it's aimed at. Turning the person to look at the form recruits that reflex to help steer attention there. A person looking straight out at the camera instead tends to hold the viewer's attention on the face itself, which is useful when the face is the point, but doesn't help point anyone toward something else on the page.
