Content & Microcopy

Microcopy is the small pieces of text throughout an interface — button labels, error messages, empty states, confirmation dialogs — that guide someone through using it, as distinct from the main body content they came to read.

Why it matters

A button labeled "Submit" doesn't tell the reader what actually happens when they click it; "Create account" does. An empty search results page that just says "No results" leaves someone stuck, while one that says "No results for 'blue jacket' — try removing a filter" gives them an actual next step. Microcopy is often the only text someone reads carefully on an entire screen, which makes vague microcopy disproportionately costly.

The core rule

Write microcopy from the reader's next action, not the system's internal state. Say what will happen if they click something, not what the system is technically doing — and for anything destructive or hard to undo, name the specific action in the confirmation, don't just ask "Are you sure?"

Good vs. bad

Good 
<h2>Delete "Q3 Report.pdf"?</h2>
<p>This can't be undone.</p>
<button>Delete file</button>
<button>Cancel</button>

The dialog names the exact file, states the consequence plainly, and the confirm button repeats the specific action instead of a generic "Yes" — there's no ambiguity about what's about to happen.

Bad 
<h2>Are you sure?</h2>
<button>Yes</button>
<button>No</button>

"Are you sure?" doesn't say what it's confirming, and "Yes" requires the reader to remember what they just clicked rather than reading the confirmation itself.

Common mistakes

  • Generic labels that don't describe the result. "Submit," "OK," and "Click here" tell the reader nothing about what happens next — a label should describe the outcome ("Send message," "Save changes").
  • Empty states with no next step. "No data" or "Nothing here yet" explains the current state but not what to do about it. A good empty state says why it's empty and what action would change that.
  • Raw error codes or technical jargon shown to users. "Error 400: Bad Request" is meaningful to a developer and meaningless to almost everyone else — it needs a plain-language translation of what went wrong and what to do next.
  • Vague confirmations for destructive actions. "Are you sure?" with generic Yes/No buttons is easy to click through without registering what's actually being confirmed. Name the action in both the message and the button.
  • Inconsistent terminology for the same concept. Calling the same thing "account" in one screen, "profile" in another, and "membership" in a third forces the reader to work out whether these are the same thing or something different.

Checklist

  • Button and link labels describe the specific result, not a generic verb.
  • Empty states explain why they're empty and suggest a next action.
  • Error messages avoid raw codes and jargon, and say what to do next.
  • Destructive-action confirmations name the specific thing being affected, in both the message and the button label.
  • The same concept is called the same thing everywhere in the product.
Go deeper

Voice vs. tone. Voice is a product's consistent personality (formal, playful, matter-of-fact) and stays constant; tone shifts with context — the same product's voice can sound reassuring in an error message and celebratory in a success message, without becoming a different voice.

Front-loading the important word. "Delete file" front-loads the consequential word; "File will be deleted" buries it at the end. In a UI a reader is scanning rather than reading fully, the first word often carries most of the weight.

Localization changes length, not just words. Microcopy that fits tightly in English (a two-word button label) can run noticeably longer in other languages — leave room in the layout rather than writing copy that only fits at exactly its original length.
